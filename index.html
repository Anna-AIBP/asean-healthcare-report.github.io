Executive summary

ASEAN’s hospitals are digitising fast. Electronic medical records, connected medical devices, telehealth and national health data platforms are transforming care. But every new connection is also a new way in for attackers, and in healthcare a successful attack does not just cost money. It can put patients at risk.

Can you keep caring for patients when systems fail?

That is the question this report is built around. The nine healthcare leaders who contributed to it, from Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand, describe a threat growing faster than their defences. Most attacks still start with a convincing message to a busy member of staff, and AI is making those messages harder to spot. Smart-hospital programmes are pulling legacy devices and outside vendors onto networks never designed for them. And AI is arriving on the wards before most organisations know how to govern it.

The biggest obstacle, however, is not technical. It sits in the boardroom. Cybersecurity generates no revenue, and many boards still treat it as a cost centre. AIBP’s 2026 survey of 603 enterprises across the region shows the same pattern: nearly half name cybersecurity, compliance and risk as a priority for their digital investments, yet far more plan to invest in AI than in cybersecurity.

The leaders making progress share a common approach. They frame cyber investment in terms the board cares about: patient safety, the cost of downtime and reputation. They make clinicians, not IT, the champions of security culture. They keep patient care first. And they have accepted that no organisation can prevent every attack. The goal is resilience: the ability to detect, contain and recover quickly, together.

One regional hospital CEO told AIBP that cybersecurity had come up only once in her two and a half years of board meetings.

The year in breaches

The leaders in this report are not describing a hypothetical. Across ASEAN, hospitals and health agencies are already being hit.

Threat researchers now rank healthcare as the most targeted industry in the Philippines, where attacks increasingly disrupt care rather than only expose data. A selection of incidents reported across the region since early 2025, including a wave of attacks on Philippine and Singapore healthcare organisations in the weeks before this report was published:

  1. Jan 2025Vietnam

    Ransomware locks a hospital information system

    The hospital information system at Hue Central Hospital’s on-demand and international treatment centre was compromised, with more than 500 GB of data encrypted and a ransom demanded.

    Source: VietNamNet

  2. Jun 2025Philippines

    A ransomware group claims patient files

    Coop Hospital in Puerto Princesa investigated a ransomware group’s claim to have taken around 45,000 files of medical data, including X-ray reports and laboratory results. The hospital said care continued without interruption.

    Source: Beyond Machines

  3. May 2026Vietnam

    Two healthcare data incidents in one month

    Vietnam’s national cybersecurity centre (A05) reported two attacks involving healthcare data during May, including diagnostic imaging data at Viet Duc University Hospital.

    Source: Tuoi Tre, via IPSIP Vietnam

  4. Jun 2026Malaysia

    Health ministry website taken offline

    Malaysia’s Ministry of Health confirmed its official website had been compromised in a cyberattack and took the portal offline while it investigated.

    Source: theSun

  5. Jul 2026Indonesia

    A teaching hospital named on a leak site

    Universitas Indonesia Hospital (RSUI) was listed on a ransomware group’s leak site, which claimed to have taken medical data. The claim has not been independently verified.

    Source: ransomware.live

  6. Oct 2026Philippines

    A third-party platform behind a hospital app is breached

    St. Luke’s Medical Center detected unauthorised access on 29 October to a platform supporting its eHealth Hub mobile app, which was managed by a third-party provider. Personal data of some patients and employees may have been exposed. The hospital said its electronic medical records and core clinical systems were not affected, took the app offline and notified the National Privacy Commission.

    Sources: Newsbytes.PH; Philstar Life

  7. Nov 2026Malaysia

    From data theft to disruption

    Malaysia’s National Cyber Security Agency warned that attackers are shifting from stealing data to disrupting physical systems, naming healthcare among the sectors at risk.

    Source: Malay Mail

  8. Nov 2026Singapore

    Six healthcare organisations hit in two weeks

    Singapore’s Ministry of Health reported unauthorised access to the systems of six healthcare and healthcare-related organisations, including Fullerton Health, Doctor Anywhere, Minmed Group and the Agency for Integrated Care. At Fullerton Health, around 140,000 migrant worker records were exposed, though no health, medical or financial data. The ministry said patient care was not disrupted and patient health records were not accessed, and asked all healthcare providers to strengthen their cybersecurity.

    Sources: The Straits Times; TNGlobal

The pattern matches what the leaders in this report describe: ransomware aimed at the hospital information system, data taken through connected platforms and public portals, and attackers who publish what they steal to force payment.

Part one6 min read

Where the risk comes from

Staff, connected devices and suppliers: the three places where care is most often disrupted.

1

Making safe behaviour easier

Most attacks still begin with a message to a busy member of staff. The hospitals making progress make the safe choice the easy one, through better systems, training and workflows.

Jojo Nufable, Vice President and Group Chief Information and Cyber Security Officer at St. Luke’s Medical Center, estimates that around 80% of attacks still come through social engineering and phishing. The phishing itself has changed. He describes emails with perfect grammar that pass straight through email security tools. Attackers research the hospital first, using news, Facebook and LinkedIn, and reference real events in their subject lines. AI has industrialised that research: what once took a skilled criminal days can now be done at scale. When a message gets past the filters, the next safeguard is a member of staff, so reporting a suspicious email has to be quick, simple and free of blame.

Cross-industry survey context

Over a quarter of enterprises (27.7%) also name AI misinformation, deepfakes and adversarial AI among their top concerns about AI.

Insiders are the second concern. In Vietnam, Thanh Son Vo, Head of Information Security at FV Hospital, points to staff with limited digital training who can be manipulated, a large market for cracked software, and a cultural instinct to “make it work” that accepts insecure shortcuts.

These weaknesses most often lead to ransomware. In a hospital, the information system and electronic medical record sit at the centre of everything: laboratory, imaging, clinical and cardiology systems all connect to them. If ransomware hits that core, patients may be harmed. As Jojo admits, no hospital is truly prepared for ransomware until the real thing arrives. The answer is constant practice. St. Luke’s moved from twice-yearly phishing simulations to monthly ones, measured by department, alongside ransomware simulations, vulnerability assessments and penetration testing.

2

The connected hospital: devices, vendors and the wider ecosystem

Smart-hospital programmes are connecting devices and suppliers that were never built for it.

Jojo describes three domains in every hospital: IT, operational technology (OT) and the Internet of Medical Things. IT is the most mature. OT is where legacy lives: devices such as infusion pumps were built to run standalone on proprietary systems. Now AI-enabled operations need that equipment connected to central systems and the cloud. Until it can be secured, St. Luke’s keeps it segmented and air-gapped. In his view, ASEAN hospitals are only starting to address OT, while hospitals in the US and Europe are well ahead.

Every connected device also brings a supplier. For Son, vendor remote access is the single biggest threat. Global medical technology brands often have no local presence in Vietnam, so hospitals depend on resellers with lower security standards to support equipment remotely. AI providers are a newer class of supplier, and smaller hospitals have little leverage to negotiate data protection terms with them.

Not all the risk comes through formal suppliers. At UP-Philippine General Hospital (UP-PGH), the country’s largest public referral hospital with over 2,000 beds and around 5,000 patients a day, resident doctors and fellows bring their own laptops and rotate out when training ends. Arnel Distor, who leads IT at UP-PGH, registers every personal device and removes access and hospital files as part of each departing doctor’s clearance.

Cross-industry survey context

Few enterprises rank suppliers or connected devices among their top cyber challenges, yet the hospital leaders interviewed see them as a front door. Legacy IT infrastructure remains the biggest barrier to digital transformation overall, cited by 42.2%.

Hospitals are responding in three ways.

Bring security into procurement and capability in-house

At UP-PGH, IT now joins equipment evaluations from the planning stage to set network and security specifications. Benedict Sulaiman, Director of IT and CTO at Mandaya Hospital Group, goes further: hospitals that outsource development also hand over control of risk, so Mandaya builds its systems in-house.

Rate every supplier by the risk it carries

Manoj Kumar Murmu, Senior Director and Head of Cybersecurity Services at Bumrungrad International Hospital, starts from business continuity. Identify the core operations and the suppliers behind them, assess the access and data each relies on, and let that risk rating decide the controls. For medical devices and software, the review draws on software bills of materials and manufacturers’ security disclosures.

Certify the ecosystem

BPJS Kesehatan, Indonesia’s national health insurer, connects to more than 27,000 health facilities. Setiaji, its Director of Information Technology, explains that facilities self-assess, BPJS verifies the result, and each earns a trust mark of up to seven stars. Partners must pass tests in a sandbox environment before they can connect.

3

Patient care first: access control without friction

No leader interviewed would let security stand between a doctor and a patient.

For Son, modern authentication largely removes the conflict. Single sign-on, multi-factor authentication and conditional access make strong controls close to seamless. The real trade-off is pace: roll changes out too fast and staff get lost; too slowly and risk stays open for years.

St. Luke’s uses a layered model. Role-based access enforces least privilege, and attribute-based access adds context, such as which clinical environment a user is in. In an emergency, doctors can “break the glass” and bypass normal protocol to save a patient. Access of this kind should be time-limited, logged and reviewed afterwards.

Cross-industry survey context

Identity is now the control enterprises lean on most, but a quarter could not say which tools they rely on.

The next challenge is identity itself. Hospitals now manage machine, bot and AI agent identities alongside people. Jojo calls unmanaged AI tools shadow AI: the new shadow IT, quietly extending the attack surface.

Part two5 min read

Leading the response

Getting the board and staff ready to keep care running.

4

Winning the boardroom

Cybersecurity generates no revenue, and most hospital boards still treat it as a cost centre.

Jojo describes the typical response: the hospital spends heavily on medical equipment, cyber is not its core business, so a small fraction of that budget should be enough. Hoo Ling Lee, Regional CEO of KPJ Healthcare, puts the underlying difficulty plainly. Expectation of return on investment is widespread across the market, while evidence of it is thin. Boards are approving budgets against a confidence that the numbers have not yet caught up with.

AI makes the conversation harder still. New attack patterns emerge faster than annual budget cycles can respond, and machine-speed attacks mean no CISO can promise the board zero incidents. The case for investment has to shift from preventing every attack to recovering from the ones that get through.

Cross-industry survey context

Half plan to spend more, and more than a quarter do not yet know. Budget timing varies across organisations, so this alone does not show how boards view cybersecurity.

The leaders who win investment change the question the board is asking.

Across the interviews, four arguments recur.

Patient safety

Jojo presents what he calls “negative ROI”: the cost of not acting, linked to patient safety and keeping clinical operations running. Son frames controls as enablers of care. Safely scanning a patient’s USB drive, for example, lets the hospital accept outside imaging instead of turning the patient away or repeating a scan.

The cost of downtime

Setiaji frames security as insurance. BPJS Kesehatan processes more than two million claims a day for a scheme covering around 98% of Indonesia’s population, so he shows management what a single hour of downtime would mean: facilities unable to verify whether patients are covered, long queues and openings for fraud. Benedict weighs a modest investment in endpoint security against a potential loss many times larger. Some organisations hit by ransomware even pay, because a ransom costs less than weeks of lost revenue, and attackers price their demands accordingly.

Real, validated risk

Manoj argues that the business case starts with risk, not spend. Risks change every year, so they must be validated continuously and linked to the hospital’s objectives, with patient trust and confidentiality as the guiding goal. A concrete threat makes the case fastest. With no in-house security specialists, Arnel proposed a managed detection and response service, tied to a major ransomware attack on the country’s national health insurer and to UP-PGH’s own appearance on a dark web listing. It was approved immediately.

Regulation

In Vietnam, new laws on AI, cybersecurity and data protection (see Chapter 7) give security leaders momentum, and Son uses compliance as the baseline for his case.

The common thread is translation. As Jojo puts it, you speak finance to the CFO, patient safety to doctors and reputation to operations.

5

Building a security culture

Leaders interviewed found security culture hardest to build among senior clinicians, and saw the best results when clinical leaders championed it.

Several leaders interviewed described senior doctors as resistant to new rules and quick to push back on warnings. Zenda Nguyen, Group COO of Tam Tri Medical Group (TMMC Healthcare), which runs seven hospitals in Vietnam, responds with a three-part approach:

  1. Clinical champions. Each hospital’s CEO and CMO act as ambassadors, so the programme is driven by the hospital rather than by central IT.
  2. Regulation as the argument. In Zenda’s experience, warnings about patient harm invite debate, while regulation is the argument doctors are most willing to accept.
  3. Discipline and SOPs. Clear procedures define what is right and wrong, backed by coordinators at group and hospital level.

Training must fit the audience: doctors, customer care staff and IT teams face different risks. Tone matters too. Instead of shaming staff who fail phishing tests, St. Luke’s now rewards those who pass, to make reporting feel safe and turn every employee into a “human firewall”.

Culture now has to cover AI as well. As AI tools reach the wards, clinicians may be tempted to shift responsibility onto a system when something goes wrong. Zenda stresses careful change management, so that accountability for clinical decisions stays with people.

Cross-industry survey context

Culture is a barrier well beyond healthcare. In AIBP’s 2026 survey, 40.3% of enterprises named organisational complexity and silos as a top challenge to digital transformation, 22.1% a lack of leadership and vision, and 18.0% strong internal resistance.

AIBP 2026 survey. All industries; Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand. See “About the evidence”.

The hardest people to reach are often outside the organisation. Setiaji can enforce controls on BPJS’s own staff, but not on health workers at partner facilities, some of whom share passwords, or on citizens using the BPJS app. Regular awareness workshops are his main tool.

Part three7 min read

What comes next

AI, regulation and quantum computing are changing the risk. Resilience is how hospitals answer it.

6

AI as defender and threat

AI is reshaping both sides of hospital security, and leaders disagree on how fast to move.

The pressure comes from attackers. AI-driven reconnaissance and machine-speed attacks are growing more sophisticated, and human teams alone cannot keep up. St. Luke’s is responding most aggressively, launching an agentic AI security operations centre in which AI agents triage, correlate and respond to incidents while human analysts govern them. Jojo expects the team to settle at around 80% AI agents and 20% humans. This is one hospital’s plan, not a benchmark, but it shows where some leaders believe security operations are heading.

Others are earlier on the journey. Setiaji’s team at BPJS Kesehatan already runs security monitoring and endpoint detection tools with a managed security operations centre. What it lacks is AI that brings them together in one view, predicts issues before they disrupt service, and pinpoints root causes.

Cross-industry survey context

Governance lags adoption. Only 8.9% have a fully embedded AI governance framework and 22.0% have none. Data privacy and security is the top concern about AI, cited by 73.4%.

That privacy concern explains why some leaders are cautious. Son notes that large organisations can negotiate data protection terms with major AI vendors, but smaller hospitals have to trust published policies. And every new technology brings new attack patterns faster than budgets can follow. He describes himself as equally excited and terrified.

Cross-industry survey context

The bigger question is governance. Zenda sees AI compliance as the main technology challenge of the next three to five years, with AI needed as a policy layer that traces how data flows within and between organisations. Manoj goes further, arguing that AI is starting to move into care itself, beginning with triage.

7

Regulation and data trust

Healthcare regulation across ASEAN is moving, but it focuses on data more than on resilience.

Vietnamdata sharing at national scale

Zenda points to the government’s goal of a national personal health record by 2030, built on the VNeID citizen app, with data flowing between hospitals and to banks and insurers. Patient trust is already fragile: Zenda describes families receiving marketing from multiple companies the day after a birth or vaccination. Her group’s answer is three layers of protection: security technology, checks that data reaches only partners the patient has approved, and controls on where data is displayed. The legal framework is tightening fast: the Law on Personal Data Protection (No. 91/2025/QH15) took effect on 1 January 2026, the Law on Artificial Intelligence (No. 134/2025/QH15) on 1 March 2026, and a revised Cybersecurity Law (No. 116/2025/QH15) on 1 July 2026. Zenda expects AI itself to become the tool that monitors and enforces data-sharing rules in real time.

Indonesiafrom no oversight to mandates

Benedict says that before COVID the Ministry of Health paid little attention to IT. Its Digital Transformation Office began setting standards during the pandemic, and the ministry has since created a dedicated health-sector cyber incident response team (Health CSIRT), which Setiaji led. According to Setiaji, health facilities must now set up their own response teams. BPJS expects connected facilities to comply with the Personal Data Protection Law (Law No. 27 of 2022, fully enforceable since October 2024) and standards such as ISO 27001 and ISO 27799. Smaller hospitals, however, may have only one or two IT staff, often reporting to finance.

Philippineshospitals that don’t talk to each other

A patient referred from a provincial hospital to a specialist centre often has tests and imaging repeated, because hospitals cannot share records. Arnel is migrating UP-PGH’s in-house medical record to the HL7 interoperability standard to make sharing possible.

Cross-industry survey context

Regulation weighs on AI more than on security. 34.2% of enterprises name regulatory and compliance challenges among their top concerns about AI, but only 10.4% name evolving compliance requirements as a top cybersecurity challenge. Talent is the other constraint: 37.0% cite a lack of talent and expertise as a barrier to digital transformation.

AIBP 2026 survey. All industries; Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand. See “About the evidence”.

Shared data, however, means shared risk. The direction for regulators is to pair data standards with mandatory resilience testing.

8

Preparing for the quantum era

Quantum computing sits further out, but hospitals need to act on it now.

No one can say when quantum computers will be able to break today’s encryption. What hospitals do know is how long their data must stay confidential, and that is the case for preparing now. Irene points out that most hospital data must be kept for around 15 years, and some medico-legal records indefinitely. Attackers do not need a quantum computer today: they can steal encrypted data now and decrypt it later.

Cross-industry survey context

Few organisations share that urgency. Only 3.0% of ASEAN enterprises name quantum computing risks among their top cybersecurity challenges.

AIBP 2026 survey. All industries; Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand. See “About the evidence”.

The Philippines has no post-quantum cryptography mandate yet. Irene sees that as a window rather than a reason to wait, since migrating encryption across a hospital network takes years. Healthway has written AI and quantum safety into its policies since 2024.

9

Resilience over perfection

The goal is not to prevent every attack, but to keep caring for patients when one gets through.

Irene describes the shift from reactive defence to proactive resilience. A reactive organisation asks whether it is protected. A resilient one asks how quickly it can keep caring for patients once it is hit. When systems go down, dialysis is delayed and results are lost, so continuity of care is the real measure. Healthway now runs exercises in which its hospital information system is assumed to be infected, to test how its business continuity plans hold. Exercises like these increasingly need to reflect AI-driven attacks.

Resilience also has to be shared, which is why Jojo formed, and now chairs, the Healthcare Cybersecurity Technology Alliance (HCTA), bringing Philippine hospitals together to lift their security posture and resilience.

Where should an organisation start? Zenda’s answer is an honest look at the gap between policy and practice.

Setiaji gives the same advice: start with an honest assessment.

Conclusion

The leaders in this report work in different countries, health systems and stages of digital maturity. Yet when asked about cybersecurity, they kept returning to the same question: can a hospital keep caring for patients when its systems fail?

Their experience shows why that question matters more each year. Hospitals are connecting more devices, more suppliers and more partners, from national health records to AI tools on the wards. Each connection can improve care, and each is another way in. As the incidents of the past year show, attacks now arrive as often through a supplier’s platform or a convincing message to a busy member of staff as through the hospital’s own systems.

The leaders making progress have changed the conversation. They speak to boards about patient safety and the cost of downtime, not IT spend. They make clinical leaders the champions of security culture. They design controls that never stand between a doctor and a patient. And they treat AI as both a defence and a risk, keeping people accountable for what it decides.

None of them claims to be safe from attack. What they share is a shift from trying to prevent every incident to preparing to recover from one: knowing their real gaps, understanding which systems and suppliers their care depends on, and practising what happens when those systems go down. Because patients and their data move between hospitals, they also see resilience as something the sector must build together, through alliances such as HCTA and closer work with regulators.

For hospital boards and executives, the lesson is clear. A cyberattack is no longer a question of if, but when.

When it comes, will your patients still be cared for?
